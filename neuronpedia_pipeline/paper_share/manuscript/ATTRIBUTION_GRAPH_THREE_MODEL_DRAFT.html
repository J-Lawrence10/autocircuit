Abstract

Similar attribution graphs may reflect a shared fact, shared wording, or a shared answer. We examined these alternatives in Gemma-2-2B, Qwen3-1.7B, and Qwen3-4B, followed by stronger controls in Qwen3-4B. Reworded questions about the same fact had higher mean feature overlap than questions about other facts in all three included samples. The positive averages survived equal-feature-count sampling, but the original comparisons did not separate facts from shared words and expected answers. A follow-up kept word and token counts identical within each of six country pairs while varying the queried country, wording, and correct A/B answer; all 48 predicted letters were correct. The same-country minus different-country overlap was 0.0628 when the answer letter stayed the same and -0.0022 when it changed. Their average, the prespecified primary outcome, was 0.0303 (95% block-bootstrap interval 0.0265–0.0338). In a separate exploratory diagnostic, all eight selected wrong-answer graphs resembled a correct response producing the same answer more than a correct response to the intended country. These results show that feature overlap alone cannot establish correct factual processing or a representation independent of the answer. The stronger controls concern one model, the failure cases share one country pair, and a failed history pilot prevented evaluation of the planned historical graph comparison.

1. Introduction

Attribution graphs describe learned features and their attributed influence for a particular prompt and output. Comparing these graphs may reveal relationships among prompts, but shared features need not mean that the same fact was processed correctly. Here we ask: Do similar attribution graphs reflect the fact being asked about—or the answer the model produces? These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. We test whether the association between shared facts and shared graph features persists when wording and answer labels are varied.

For example, two questions about the capital of France can preserve the requested fact, country name, and expected answer while changing the sentence structure. Their graphs could therefore overlap for several reasons. We measure the overlap between sets of learned features, not similarity between all graph connections. This distinction matters: a feature-set comparison can identify an association without recovering a reasoning pathway or establishing a causal mechanism. Interventions are required to test mechanistic hypotheses suggested by attribution graphs [1].

We proceed through three comparisons. First, we ask whether reworded questions about the same fact share more features than questions about other facts in the available three-model samples. Second, after examining the original controls, we hold the available words constant and vary the queried country and answer letter in Qwen3-4B. Third, we compare selected wrong-answer graphs with correct responses that preserve either the intended country or the produced answer. This final comparison tests the interpretation of graph resemblance, not how frequently errors occur. Prior studies address prompt-specific circuits [2], multiple-choice selection [5], and positional retrieval [7]; our focus is what feature overlap can establish about factual processing.

2.1 Cohorts, provenance, and completion

Three models from two families are represented: Gemma-2-2B, Qwen3-1.7B, and Qwen3-4B. All graphs were generated remotely through Neuronpedia; the present analyses use archived inputs and CPU computation only. No local model inference was performed. Model, exporter, decomposition, pruning, and available prompt coverage vary together, so cross-model differences are pipeline comparisons rather than isolated architecture or scaling effects.

The original design contains 30 facts, each with a starting question, a factual rewording, and two controls using made-up names, divided into development and evaluation sets. Llama was deferred for resource reasons before extension outcomes and was not generated.

Collection and inclusion counts are separated in the sample-accounting table. All three models contribute chemistry comparisons; geography and history do not provide complete three-model comparisons. Unused questions are not automatically incorrect answers: missing controls, partial next tokens, and lack of a comparison partner are distinct exclusion reasons.

2.2 Representation and controlled outcome

We use sets of unique (feature family, layer, feature id) identities, collapsed across token positions. Reconstruction errors, embeddings, logits, and ambiguous identifiers are excluded from the primary representation. Feature namespaces are kept distinct and never compared across models. This measures feature composition, not recovery of graph topology or complete input-to-output pathways.

For a starting question T_i and its factual rewording P_i, the primary difference in feature overlap is:

M_i = Jaccard(T_i, P_i) - mean_j Jaccard(T_i, P_j),

where j includes every other included fact in the same model, subject, and development or evaluation set. Inclusion requires all four prompt/control graphs, both factual prompts matching the expected highest-probability next token after trimming whitespace, and another qualifying fact for comparison. This is a next-token matching rule, not verification of a complete generated answer.

In plain language, this subtracts average overlap with reworded questions about other facts from overlap with a reworded question about the same fact. Positive values mean more shared features in the same-fact comparison; zero means equal overlap; negative values mean more shared features in the other-fact comparison. It is a difference between Jaccard scores, not a percentage increase. Other designs below specify their own subtraction; weighted cosine comparisons are called differences in weighted similarity rather than feature overlap.

The original uncertainty estimates use 10,000 fact-bootstrap resamples and exact one-sided sign flips. Complementary within-domain fact-label permutations assess matched assignment without treating graph pairs as independent samples. Facts reuse comparators, sign-flip assumptions require care, and the same facts recur across models: 8+5+10 is not 23 unique independent facts. No pooled model-ranking test is performed.

2.3 Crossed design and controls

Gemma and Qwen3-4B each have three subjects, three facts per subject, and three formulations per fact in the fact-by-wording experiment. We compare questions sharing a fact but not the sentence pattern, or sharing the pattern but not the fact, against questions differing in both. Differences are calculated within subjects and averaged across subjects. Permutations preserve this crossed design. The relative ordering of the differences is descriptive, not a test that one factor dominates or that models differ.

The two made-up-name controls, also called nonce controls, replace real names while retaining either the starting sentence pattern or its reworded version. A separate sensitivity check compares outputs only within broad next-token categories: letters/numbers, whitespace, or punctuation/symbols. This does not match the exact answer, its meaning, or its probability.

2.4 Exploratory confound checks

All 342 available raw graph files were hash-verified during the new extraction. Collision-safe feature counts agreed with the archived audits, and the 23 primary fact margins were reconstructed to absolute tolerance 1e-12.

Feature counts. Within each model/domain/held-out block, K is the smallest target or positive-paraphrase feature count. Each graph is independently subsampled without replacement to K or floor(K/2). Across 200 repetitions (seed 20260905), a sampled graph is reused in all its comparisons. We report per-fact averaged margins, descriptive 10,000-resample fact-bootstrap intervals, and separately the variability across subsampling repetitions. Equal cardinality does not equalize feature-selection bias or remove lexical confounding.

Shared words and answer identity. Word overlap is case-folded alphanumeric word-set Jaccard, without stopword removal. Other-fact comparators must either equal or exceed own-paraphrase word overlap, fall within an absolute 0.05 caliper, or satisfy that caliper plus a feature-count ratio within 1.25 of the own paraphrase. All qualifying comparators are averaged. No support is reported as not estimable, not zero. Calipers were not widened after inspection. Expected-answer identity is separately tabulated.

Tokenizer audit. Official Qwen tokenizer revisions were pinned and their files hashed. Their single-token decodings were checked against archived prompt pieces and top logit IDs. Expected continuations were encoded against each stored prompt with zero or one separating space, requiring prefix stability. Whole-answer next-token matches, substantive multi-token prefixes, whitespace-only compatibility, and mismatches under those conventions remain distinct. Historical tokenizer revision identity is not fully recoverable, and current-tokenizer compatibility is not proof of a completed answer. Gemma’s official tokenizer download returned HTTP 401; its tokenizer-aware classification remains unavailable. No mirror or local weights were used [3,4].

For crossed output sensitivity, a domain must retain all nine cells under the original strict string rule. We do not select individual favorable cells or replace the original cohort.

2.5 Matched-choice follow-up separating queried country and answer label

To separate the requested fact from the answer label more directly, we tested Qwen3-4B using its non-thinking chat format. Within each of six country pairs, we varied the country asked about, the assignment of capitals to A/B, and the wording, producing eight graphs per pair. Each prompt contained the same words and the same counts of tokenizer IDs within its pair, although order and country roles changed. France/Germany was used only in the preliminary behavioral check for this experiment. We measured same-country minus different-country overlap separately for comparisons with the same and different answer letters. The prespecified primary outcome was the average of these two differences. Uncertainty used six country pairs, not 48 independent graphs. The exact conditional fact-correspondence test contained 4096 assignments; its locally frozen multiple-testing adjustment covered three model/version opportunities.

The follow-up design was informed by earlier results, but its rules were fixed before collecting its evaluation graphs. Earlier plain-completion pilots had failed: Qwen3-4B chose A on all eight items, giving 4/8 correct labels, while Gemma produced the correct capitals but none of the eight requested letters. These failed pilots remain separate from the original cohorts. The revised Qwen study controls word and token inventories and varies answer labels, but does not remove differences in query order or how country names are assigned roles.

2.6 Position pilot and exploratory failure-case diagnostic

A separate follow-up asked about a country’s position in a list while varying the queried country, list order, answer mapping, and wording. Its prespecified accuracy requirement had to be met in a 16-item pilot before collecting evaluation graphs. After the pilot failed, we conducted an explicitly exploratory diagnostic of selected errors; this was not a replacement evaluation cohort.

The diagnostic contained 20 next-token previews: four literal/replay positive controls and four selected France/Germany cases varied by how the country was identified (its name or list position) and by response format (A/B letter or capital name). These cases were selected because the correct answer conflicted with choosing an answer option by its position. We fixed the 16 diagnostic prompts before generating their graphs. Each wrong-answer graph was compared with two correct-answer graphs: one for the intended country and one producing the same answer as the error. Both controls preserved the option layout and country-list order. The position-based and named-country prompts differ in wording, length, and repeated names; unlike the matched-choice experiment, their word and token inventories are not identical.

Graph settings were maxNLogits=10, desiredLogitProb=0.99, nodeThreshold=0.8, edgeThreshold=0.85, and maxFeatureNodes=5000, using hosted Qwen3-4B with the recorded transcoder source. Exact prompts and token sequences, source/settings, checksums, identifier integrity, and observed answers were audited. Wrong answers were deliberately retained. All comparisons report feature-set Jaccard, exported-influence-weighted cosine, and an equal-feature-count sensitivity using 200 repetitions with fixed seeds. Direct incoming edges to the observed answer logit were summarized by source type and token location, preserving signs and absolute weights. Display panels retain the ten largest absolute direct edges and the ten largest upstream edges into those displayed predecessors; full tables retain the remaining edges.

These selected cases concern two facts in one country pair, with reused controls. We report descriptive comparisons without confirmatory p-values or population confidence intervals. The country-location weight ratio discussed below was an additional descriptive inspection of the archived role tables, not a prespecified primary endpoint. All outcomes concern the top next token under the hosted non-thinking setup; they do not independently establish full generated-answer behavior [6]. Local provenance checks also do not independently certify hosted-weight identity.

2.7 Final saved-data audit and separate history follow-up

After inspecting the matched-choice results, we directly compared the same-label and changed-label fact contrasts across the six country-pair blocks. We report the mean paired difference, a 20,000-resample block-bootstrap interval, a Student-t interval, and a two-sided exact block sign-flip test. These summaries assume independent blocks; the sign-flip null additionally assumes symmetric block contrasts. This added test is exploratory and is distinct from the original conditional correspondence test. The 4,096 correspondence assignments are null configurations within six blocks, not 4,096 independent observations.

As graph-location sensitivities, we removed feature nodes at the final prediction context position, in the last quarter of observed feature layers, or both, before collapsing feature identities. Empty representations are unavailable rather than assigned zero overlap. These filters test concentration of the measured association; they are not causal ablations, and retained graphs remain conditioned on the generated output. We applied the same filters to the selected failure comparisons without attaching population p-values to their dependent triples.

A final historical-date study was locally frozen on 5 October before its hosted outcomes. It used Qwen3-4B only, two separate pilot event pairs plus four literal-label checks, and a planned evaluation of 20 event pairs spanning US documentary history and spaceflight chronology. All eight prompts per pair had identical token inventories; queried event, year-to-label mapping, and question/context order varied. The protocol required all 20 pilot checks to pass before any of the 160 evaluation graphs could be requested. Its planned primary endpoint was the mean same-label minus changed-label fact contrast, conditional on complete correct-answer blocks, with at least 16 supported blocks including six per theme. The failed pilot stopped collection, so this endpoint was not measured. This prospective, outcome-informed follow-up does not change original history inclusion.

3. Results

3.1 Reworded questions about the same fact share more features

In the included evaluation questions, rewordings of the same fact shared more features than rewordings of other facts in the same subject and evaluation set. Mean differences in feature overlap were 0.170 for Gemma (95% interval [0.085, 0.276], n=8), 0.085 for Qwen3-1.7B ([0.076, 0.095], n=5), and 0.162 for available Qwen3-4B cases ([0.131, 0.194], n=10). All original question-level differences were positive. Exact sign-flip p-values were 0.00390625, 0.03125, and 0.0009765625; fact-label permutation p-values were 0.00139, 0.00833, and 0.0000694. These results establish an association within the selected samples, not an explanation for it. Shared facts recur across models, subject coverage differs, and the original Qwen3-4B extension remains incomplete.

3.2 Original controls do not separate facts from shared words and answers

No evaluation question had an other-fact comparison meeting any of the three word-matching or joint word/feature-count rules: 0/8 Gemma, 0/5 Qwen3-1.7B, and 0/10 Qwen3-4B. Even the most word-overlapping other-fact prompt scored at least 0.196 below the same-fact rewording in Gemma and Qwen3-1.7B, and at least 0.181 below it in Qwen3-4B. The lack of a matching control was therefore not a borderline threshold result.

None of the 86 directed other-fact comparisons shared the expected answer, whereas same-fact pairs did. The original comparison therefore changes factual content, word overlap, and expected-answer identity together. It cannot isolate an association with factual meaning that is independent of words and answers. This does not show that words explain the entire result; it identifies a comparison the original data cannot make.

In the fact-by-wording experiment, feature-overlap differences for shared facts and shared sentence patterns were 0.074/0.118 in Gemma and 0.101/0.064 in Qwen3-4B. Differences in influence-weighted similarity had the same ordering within each pipeline (0.090/0.114 and 0.105/0.075). However, these cohorts include incomplete or mismatched predicted answers. Only Qwen3-4B geography passed the strict expected-token rule in all nine questions; detailed counts appear in Supporting analysis A1. The differing average ordering does not establish a statistically significant model difference or universal dominance of fact or wording.

When controls were matched only by broad next-token category, same-pattern made-up-name differences were -0.069 (Gemma, n=2), -0.014 (Qwen3-1.7B, n=4), and +0.096 (Qwen3-4B, n=10). Reworded made-up-name differences were positive in all three cohorts. Thus retaining the sentence pattern did not consistently remove the higher overlap with the factual rewording. Together, these controls motivate a follow-up that preserves the available words while varying the requested country and the answer letter.

3.3 The overlap difference depends on the answer letter

We kept the available words and token counts identical within each country pair while varying the country asked about, the wording, and the correct A/B answer. We then compared same-country and different-country overlap separately when the answer letter stayed the same and when it changed. All 48 evaluation decisions matched the intended A/B label, and all exports passed the recorded identifier and checksum checks. This experiment concerns Qwen3-4B only, not a three-model replication of the stronger control.

The same-country minus different-country overlap was 0.0628 with the same answer letter and -0.0022 with a changed letter. Their average, the prespecified primary outcome, was 0.0303 (95% block-bootstrap interval 0.0265–0.0338), positive in each of six country-pair blocks. The exact conditional correspondence p-value was 0.000244141, or 0.000732422 after the prespecified three-opportunity adjustment. The changed-letter estimate is near zero, but was not an equivalence test. A positive average across the two conditions does not establish an answer-independent factual representation.

For comparison, the descriptive same-letter minus different-letter overlap, averaged across country relationships, was 0.2827. This larger difference is not a fraction of variance or reasoning explained. The comparisons have different structures, and word order, answer mapping, and country roles remain possible explanations.

The exploratory direct paired difference between these two components was 0.0650 Jaccard units across six blocks (95% block-bootstrap interval 0.0582–0.0715; Student-t interval 0.0553–0.0746; two-sided block sign-flip p=0.03125). All six differences were positive. The difference remained 0.0720 after excluding final-position feature nodes, 0.0688 after excluding the last quarter of observed feature layers, and 0.0732 after both filters. Thus the measured contrast is not confined to the removed nodes, but these observational filters do not establish answer-independent processing elsewhere in the model. See Supplement Figure S6 and the saved-data audit.

3.4 Selected wrong-answer graphs resemble correct responses with the same answer

For example, with options “A: Berlin. B: Paris” and countries “France, Germany,” asking for the first country’s capital produced A or Berlin, depending on the response format. Naming France instead produced B or Paris; naming Germany produced A or Berlin. The wrong-answer graph was more similar to the correct Germany response producing the same answer than to the correct France response. This tests what resemblance means when the requested fact is answered incorrectly; it does not identify the cause of the error.

The separate list-position pilot produced 8/16 correct A/B decisions and failed its prespecified accuracy requirement, so no evaluation graph cohort was collected under that protocol. A local audit found no mismatch in expected answers, submitted prompts, token sequences, or top-token decoding. The later 20-preview diagnostic passed all four literal/replay checks. All eight named-country prompts produced the intended next token, whereas the eight selected position-based prompts did not, across both response formats. The selected cases motivate the graph comparison below; they neither estimate general error rates nor establish a zero effect for the uncollected position-balanced experiment.

All 16 diagnostic graphs passed integrity checks and reproduced their archived preview answers. Thus all eight ordinal errors persisted, all eight named-country controls remained correct, and all eight comparison triples met the clean-export and output-matching requirements. In every triple, the failure graph shared more features with the correct same-output response than with the correct intended-country response (Figure 6). The mean same-output minus same-country Jaccard difference was 0.1983 for A/B responses and 0.2649 for capital-name responses. Equal-feature-count sensitivities retained differences of 0.1851 and 0.2361, respectively; influence-weighted cosine preserved the direction in all eight triples. These are dependent descriptive comparisons, not eight independent replications.

The graph-location audit preserved the same-output advantage in every selected comparison under all three filters. Removing both final-position nodes and the last quarter of observed feature layers gave mean differences of 0.2053 for labels and 0.2608 for capital names. This rules out confinement of the observed difference to those removed node sets, not output conditioning of the remaining graph (Supplement Figure S6).

These deliberately selected layouts cannot distinguish matching an option by position from mishandling the negated position in the question. The intended correct-answer logit is absent from the error exports, so its attribution is unavailable, not zero. Supporting analysis A3 retains the source-location observations and approximation-error measurements; none identifies a causal error mechanism.

3.6 The history pilot limits extension beyond the tested geography task

The matched-choice result does not automatically extend to other subjects. The final historical-date pilot tested whether the planned graph comparison had sufficient behavioral support to proceed.

The fixed 20-preview pilot finished with 15 correct top-label decisions: 4/4 literal A/B instruction checks, 7/8 document-history cells, and 4/8 spaceflight-history cells. All normalized returned prompts matched the submitted prompts, and server token inventories matched within each historical block. The protocol required 20/20 correct decisions, so no evaluation graphs were requested and the planned historical graph-overlap contrast was not measured.

The document-history error occurred when A represented 1783 and B represented 1776: the question asked for the year of adoption of the US Declaration of Independence, with the Treaty of Paris as context, but the top label was A. Reversing question/context order in that mapping returned B. Such variation does not establish a specific causal failure mechanism or absence of historical knowledge. The two selected pilot pairs are not a history-accuracy benchmark. This outcome limits the available replication rather than revising the earlier graph results; the original history exclusions remain unchanged.

4. Discussion

The three sets of comparisons support a common measurement conclusion. Reworded questions about the same fact share more features in the included samples, but those prompts also share words and expected answers. The stronger Qwen3-4B controls preserve word and token inventories while varying the requested country and answer letter. They retain a positive average difference, but its same-letter and changed-letter components show that this average is not evidence of answer-independent factual overlap. The selected errors illustrate the consequence: an incorrect response can resemble a correct response producing the same answer more than a correct response to the intended country.

The result is not a choice between facts having no representation and graphs being useless. Feature overlap describes relationships among these prompts and outputs; it does not determine which relationship caused the overlap. Fact-by-wording and made-up-name controls vary by subject and pipeline, and many original outputs are incomplete or fail the expected-token rule. Likewise, equal feature counts and graph-location filters narrow some explanations without separating model organization from the decomposition, pruning, and output-conditioned graph construction.

The selected capital-name errors offer an exploratory clue about choosing the intended country: their source-location weights favor the other country, while named-country controls favor the intended one. That pattern is not consistent across A/B responses. Incorrect reference selection, option-position matching, mishandled negation, and graph structure tied to the produced answer remain alternatives. Fixed-attention attribution graphs also need not explain how the relevant attention pattern arose [1]. In relation to prior work on selection and positional-retrieval vulnerabilities [5,7], the present diagnostic shows an interpretation limit of feature overlap rather than a new established failure mechanism.

The scope of the evidence is uneven. Three models from two families contribute to the original comparison, but only Qwen3-4B contributes to the stronger controls and selected-error diagnostic. The six country pairs supply replication for the matched-choice estimates; the failure cases share one country pair and overlapping controls. The original extension is incomplete, and the failed historical pilot provides no historical graph-overlap estimate. Next-token outcomes under one hosted setup do not independently establish full generated-answer behavior or replication across deployments.

A causal explanation would require interventions on nominated features, while distinguishing list-position matching from negation errors would require separately varying those factors and testing new entities. Those are requirements for stronger claims, not evidence provided here. The present contribution is a bounded evaluation of feature overlap: comparisons must account for shared wording, expected or produced answers, and behavioral support before similarity can inform an interpretation of factual processing.

5. Conclusion

Reworded questions about the same fact share more graph features in the analyzed samples. Stronger Qwen3-4B controls show that the difference also depends on whether the answer letter stays the same, and selected wrong-answer graphs resemble correct responses producing that same answer. Together, these findings show why feature overlap alone cannot establish correct factual processing or a representation independent of the answer. They do not show that factual knowledge is absent or that a causal circuit has been recovered.

A3 Source-location observations in selected errors

An additional inspection of token-location summaries provided a narrower clue. In all four capital-name failures, feature sources at the wrong country’s name in the list had 4.9–14.6 times the absolute direct incoming weight of sources at the intended country’s name. In all four named-country capital controls, the intended country’s name had greater weight. The corresponding A/B summaries did not exhibit a consistent wrong-country dominance. This format-dependent observation is compatible with incorrect reference selection, but token location does not establish feature semantics or causal necessity.

The graphs also leave substantial uncertainty. Reconstruction-error sources accounted for a median 23.1% of absolute direct incoming weight to the observed logit across the 16 graphs (range 17.2–31.4%). The intended correct logit was absent from all eight failure exports, so its attribution is unavailable rather than zero. Most displayed nodes occur at late layers near the answer-generation position; a “chat frame” location label does not imply a formatting mechanism. Crucially, these selected layouts confound choosing the answer option at the requested position with choosing the country named by the excluded ordinal in phrases such as “first country, not the second.” Position matching and mishandled negation therefore remain observationally indistinguishable in this batch.
